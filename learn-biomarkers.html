Biomarker Reference Guide
Understanding the key hormones and markers that shape your fertility, cycle health, and aging trajectory
verifiedEducational article · Clinical review pending · 8 min read
Why Biomarkers Matter

Your reproductive hormones tell a deeply personal story. They reflect ovarian reserve, cycle quality, metabolic health, and aging trajectory. Tracking them over time — not just looking at single lab results — reveals patterns that no isolated blood draw can capture. This guide covers the seven key markers referenced in the Ovuluna protocol and the Ovarian Research Framework.

🧬
FSH — Follicle-Stimulating Hormone
Follicular phase: 3.5–12.5 mIU/mL · Peak (ovulation): 4.7–21.5 mIU/mL · Luteal: 1.7–7.7 mIU/mL
Secreted by the pituitary to recruit ovarian follicles each cycle. Rising FSH (especially >10–12 mIU/mL on cycle day 3) signals declining ovarian reserve. The "FSH test" (CD3) is a standard fertility workup. In menopause, FSH climbs above 25 mIU/mL.
🌱
AMH — Anti-Müllerian Hormone
Age 25–30: 2.0–6.8 ng/mL · Age 35–39: 0.7–4.2 ng/mL · Age 40–44: 0.2–2.0 ng/mL
Produced by granulosa cells of growing follicles. AMH is the single best blood marker of ovarian reserve because it can be measured at any cycle day. AMH < 1.0 ng/mL suggests diminished ovarian reserve (DOR). AMH < 0.5 ng/mL strongly predicts a short fertility window. It declines naturally with age and does not fluctuate across the menstrual cycle.
🌸
Estradiol (E2)
Early follicular: 20–80 pg/mL · Late follicular (peak): 200–500 pg/mL · Luteal: 60–200 pg/mL
The dominant form of estrogen. E2 peaks just before ovulation, triggering the LH surge and making cervical mucus fertile. Low E2 in the follicular phase can impair follicle growth and endometrial thickness. In perimenopause, E2 becomes erratic, then low in postmenopause (<20 pg/mL).
⚡
LH — Luteinizing Hormone
Follicular: 2–12 mIU/mL · Ovulation surge: 25–100+ mIU/mL · Luteal: 1–11 mIU/mL
LH surges dramatically ~24–36 hours before ovulation — the basis for LH ovulation predictor kits (OPKs). A ratio of LH:FSH > 2–3 on CD3 can suggest PCOS. LH is also elevated in menopause (≥25 mIU/mL).
🌙
Progesterone (P4)
Follicular: < 1.5 ng/mL · Mid-luteal: 5–20 ng/mL · Peak (7 dpo): 10–25 ng/mL
Progesterone rises after ovulation and is produced by the corpus luteum. A mid-luteal level > 3–5 ng/mL confirms ovulation occurred. Progesterone thickens the endometrium for implantation, raises body temperature (BBT shift), and modulates mood and sleep.
🦋
TSH — Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone
Optimal for fertility: 0.5–2.5 mIU/L · Normal range: 0.4–4.0 mIU/L
Thyroid health is deeply intertwined with ovarian function. Even subclinical hypothyroidism (TSH > 2.5) is associated with ovulatory dysfunction and early pregnancy loss. TSH typically rises in menopause, contributing to metabolic slowing.
🍬
HbA1C — Glycated Hemoglobin
Optimal: < 5.0% · Normal: < 5.7% · Pre-diabetes: 5.7–6.4% · Diabetes: ≥ 6.5%
HbA1C reflects average blood glucose over the past 2–3 months. Elevated glucose causes follicular oxidative stress, accelerating ovarian aging. The GeroScience paper (2024) identifies glycemic control as a key modifiable factor for preserving ovarian reserve. HbA1C < 5.0% is associated with a lower rate of follicular atresia.
