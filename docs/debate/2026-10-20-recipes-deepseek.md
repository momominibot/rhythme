We reviewed this as a joint clinical panel: an accredited dietitian and an obstetrician-gynaecologist in Singapore. We are not commenting on style, and we are not approving the copy.

## 1. Errors of fact or evidence

“PCOS is now also called PMOS.”
This is an overstatement. The 2023 International PCOS Guideline cited in the app still uses polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS). PMOS is used by some patient education resources and advocates, but it is not an internationally accepted replacement. Suggest: “PCOS is still the formal term; some resources now also use the proposed name polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS).”

“There, more yeses went with better cholesterol, blood sugar and waistlines.”
This overstates the cited PREDIMED validation paper. Higher MEDAS scores were associated with higher HDL cholesterol, lower triglycerides, lower fasting glucose, and lower BMI and waist circumference. That is not the same as “better cholesterol” broadly, and it was a cross-sectional association, not proof. Suggest: “In that study, higher scores were associated with more favourable HDL cholesterol, triglycerides and blood glucose.”

“Food does not stop hot flushes.”
This is broader than the evidence. The Menopause Society statement does not recommend soy or a special diet for hot flushes, but it does not test every food or dietary pattern. Some recognised hot flush triggers include hot drinks, spicy food and alcohol. Suggest: “No specific food or soy has been shown to stop hot flushes; if flushes are bothersome, talk to your doctor about treatment.”

No other factual lines need correction.

## 2. Safety and inclusion

Because the app is used by women who may be pregnant, have diabetes, kidney disease, thyroid disease, food allergies, or a history of eating disorder, these need to be visible on the recipe screen, not buried.

“Say Yes To Lower-Calorie Hawker Foods”
The card shows this as a headline. The app says it avoids weight-loss pressure and weight stigma, but “Lower-Calorie” in the headline is weight and calorie framing. For women with eating disorders this is exactly the kind of label that can be harmful. Suggest displaying it as “HealthHub: Hawker Meals” with the app line “Lighter picks at the hawker centre, dish by dish.” If the HPB source title must appear, keep it behind the link.

“Cannellini beans and tuna with herbs: protein and fibre in ten minutes.”
Tuna can be fresh or canned, and fresh tuna is higher in mercury. If this is canned tuna, say so. If a user is pregnant, there needs to be a mercury and raw fish warning. Suggest: “Beans and drained canned tuna with herbs: protein and fibre in ten minutes. If pregnant, follow HPB’s fish advisory; avoid raw tuna.”

“Ricotta, milk and parmesan with spinach: calcium for bones.”
Two issues. First, unpasteurised soft cheese is a listeria risk in pregnancy. Second, many Singaporean adults are lactose intolerant or avoid dairy. Suggest: “Ricotta, milk and parmesan with spinach: calcium for bones. If pregnant, use pasteurised ricotta and cook until piping hot. If you avoid dairy, use calcium-fortified soy alternatives.”

“calcium when the tofu label says calcium-set”
This is not reliable in Singapore. Tofu labels usually list “calcium sulphate” as a coagulant; many do not use the phrase “calcium-set.” Suggest: “calcium if the ingredients include calcium sulphate.”

Soy and thyroid or breast cancer
Whole soy foods in usual food amounts are generally safe, including for breast cancer survivors. High-dose concentrated isoflavone supplements are a different issue and should not be recommended. Soy can also interfere with levothyroxine absorption. Add: “If you take thyroid medication, take it with water and separate it from soy, calcium or iron by at least one hour, or as advised by your doctor.”

Kidney disease
Several recipes are very high in beans, nuts, dairy and fish. For women with chronic kidney disease, these can be too high in potassium, phosphate or protein. Add: “If you have kidney disease, check with your renal dietitian before increasing beans, nuts, dairy or fish.”

Fruit juice and diabetes
The Mediterranean check says “Fresh juice counts.” For PCOS or diabetes, fruit juice can raise blood glucose quickly and is not a substitute for whole fruit. This also does not match HPB’s preference for whole fruit. Suggest changing item 4 to: “Three or more pieces of whole fruit a day? Whole fruit is best; if you drink juice, keep it to one small glass and do not count it as multiple servings.”

Allergies
The app should add one visible line: “Recipes contain common allergens, including dairy, egg, soy, fish, tree nuts, peanuts, sesame and gluten. Check the source recipe before cooking.”

## 3. The Mediterranean check

The 13 items are mostly faithful to MEDAS. The wording is close to the original criteria, and the serving sizes are sensible. Removing the wine question is clinically correct. Alcohol is not something to take up for health, and the NHS advice to avoid alcohol while trying to get pregnant is right.

However, there are Singapore-specific problems.

“About 4 tablespoons of olive oil a day or more”
This is the PREDIMED threshold, not a personal target. Four tablespoons is roughly 480 kcal, which is high in a Singapore context where the same app points to lower-calorie hawker choices. Add: “This is the level seen in the Mediterranean study, not a daily goal for everyone.”

“Fresh juice counts”
This should be changed, as above. MEDAS counts fresh fruit juice in its original scoring, but for a PCOS and diabetes-aware app, whole fruit is safer and more aligned with HPB.

“Less than one sweet or fizzy drink a day”
The original MEDAS item is about sugar-sweetened beverages, not all fizzy drinks. Plain sparkling water and diet soft drinks are not the target. Suggest: “Less than one sugar-sweetened drink a day? Bubble tea, sweetened kopi and teh, soft drinks, cordials and fruit drinks count.”

“Fish or seafood three or more times a week”
This needs a pregnancy qualifier. If pregnant, women should choose cooked low-mercury fish, follow HPB’s fish advisory, and avoid raw or undercooked shellfish.

No score bands is the right choice. Once the wine item is removed, the tool no longer produces the validated 14-point MEDAS score. Do not call it a score. “Use it as a mirror and a list of ideas” is appropriate.

Source: NICE heavy menstrual bleeding guideline.

Source: standard individualised dietary advice; no single guideline replaces clinical review.

## Mediterranean check
Intro: These questions come from MEDAS, a short checklist from PREDIMED, a Spanish heart study of older adults. There, more yeses went with better cholesterol, blood sugar and waistlines. It was not built for fertility or the ovaries, so use it as a mirror of your week and a list of ideas, not a score to chase.
Wine note: The original has a 14th question that gives a point for drinking 7 or more glasses of wine a week. Rhythme leaves it out: alcohol is not something to take up for health, and the NHS advises none when you are trying to get pregnant.
Drinks tip: No recipe for this one: water, plain tea or coffee, or kopi-o kosong instead.
Items:
1. Is olive oil your main cooking fat?
2. About 4 tablespoons of olive oil a day or more, counting cooking, salads and meals out?
3. Two or more servings of vegetables a day, at least one raw or as a salad? [A serving is about 200 g; a side dish counts as half.]
4. Three or more pieces of fruit a day? Fresh juice counts.
5. Less than one serving a day of red meat, burgers or processed meat such as ham or sausage? [A serving is 100 to 150 g.]
6. Less than one serving a day of butter, margarine or cream? [A serving is 12 g.]
7. Less than one sweet or fizzy drink a day? [Bubble tea, sweetened kopi and teh, and soft drinks all count.]
8. Beans, lentils or chickpeas three or more times a week? [A serving is 150 g.]
9. Fish or seafood three or more times a week? [A serving is 100 to 150 g of fish, or 200 g of shellfish.]
10. Shop-bought cakes, biscuits or pastries fewer than three times a week?
11. Nuts three or more times a week? Peanuts count. [A serving is 30 g, about a small handful.]
12. Do you usually choose chicken or turkey over beef, pork, burgers or sausages?
13. Twice a week or more, a dish cooked with a sauce of tomato with onion, leek or garlic, simmered in olive oil?
