# 1. Errors of fact or evidence

- **“PCOS is now also called PMOS.”**  
  This is not an accepted international rename in the 2023 PCOS guideline or current clinical practice. A single Jean Hailes page dated 2026 cannot establish that PCOS “is now also called” PMOS. Do not present PMOS as a recognised synonym or replacement.  
  **Replacement:** “Some resources are beginning to use the term PMOS, but PCOS remains the standard clinical name used in Singapore and in the 2023 international guideline.”

- **“PCOS has been renamed polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS)” in source E75.**  
  This is presented as established fact, but it is not supported by the cited 2023 international guideline. It may confuse users searching for care, insurance claims or medication information. If retained, label it explicitly as terminology used by that resource, not a global rename.

- **“There, more yeses went with better cholesterol, blood sugar and waistlines.”**  
  This is broadly supported by the cited cross-sectional MEDAS analyses, but it implies a clinically meaningful effect from answering the screener. MEDAS was developed as a brief adherence tool, and the observed associations do not show that changing answers improves cholesterol, glucose or waist size.  
  **Replacement:** “In that study, higher scores were associated with better HDL cholesterol, lower fasting glucose and lower BMI or waist circumference. These are associations, not proof that changing a checklist score changes health.”

- **“The original has a 14th question that gives a point for drinking 7 or more glasses of wine a week.”**  
  Incorrect. MEDAS item 14 awards a point for consuming **seven or more servings of wine per week**, where a serving is defined as one glass. It is not a recommendation to drink seven glasses weekly. The wording risks making MEDAS sound absurd and may misrepresent alcohol evidence.  
  **Replacement:** “The original 14th item awards a point for drinking seven or more glasses of wine per week. Rhythme omits it; no one should start drinking alcohol for health.”

- **“water, plain tea or coffee, or kopi-o kosong instead.”**  
  Kopi-o kosong is plain black coffee without sugar, so it is reasonable, but “plain tea or coffee” should specify no added sugar, condensed milk or evaporated milk if it is intended as a replacement for sugary drinks.  
  **Replacement:** “Water, or plain tea or coffee without sugar, such as kopi-o kosong or teh-o kosong.”

- **“Three or more pieces of fruit a day? Fresh juice counts.”**  
  This follows MEDAS, but it conflicts with Singapore and NHS public-health messaging because fruit juice is lower in satiety and can be high in free sugars. It is not factually wrong as a translation of MEDAS, but it should not be presented as general healthy-eating advice.  
  **Replacement:** “For this checklist, 100% fruit juice counts as MEDAS originally scored it, but whole fruit is the preferred everyday choice.”

- **“Red rice, roast vegetable & aduki bean salad | Wholegrain red rice and beans in place of white rice: more fibre in every spoonful.”**  
  Nutritionally reasonable, but “in place of white rice” may be unnecessarily prescriptive and could feel stigmatising to users whose staple is white rice. HPB supports replacing at least part of the staple with wholegrains, not necessarily all of it.  
  **Replacement:** “Wholegrain red rice and beans add fibre; try them in place of some or all white rice.”

- **“Sweetened with dates and maple syrup, so a small bowl.”**  
  This is sensible, but dates and maple syrup are still sources of free or added sugars and should not be framed as inherently “balanced” because they are less refined. The line is acceptable if portion guidance remains.

- **“calcium for bones” and “calcium and omega-3 fats from one tin.”**  
  These are nutrient claims, not treatment claims, and are acceptable only if the actual recipes and canned-salmon quantities provide meaningful calcium. Users should not assume all canned salmon contains edible bones or the same calcium content.  
  **Replacement:** “Canned salmon with soft, edible bones can provide calcium, along with omega-3 fats.”

- **“Sardines with tomato & green beans | Canned sardines bring omega-3 fats, and their soft bones bring calcium.”**  
  Same qualification: only sardines eaten with soft edible bones contribute meaningful calcium.  
  **Replacement:** “Canned sardines provide omega-3 fats; eating the soft bones adds calcium.”

# 2. Safety and inclusion

## Eating disorders, weight pressure and stigma

- **“Lighter picks at the hawker centre, dish by dish.”** and guide title **“Say Yes To Lower-Calorie Hawker Foods”**  
  Calorie-focused language can be harmful for users with disordered eating and is unnecessary in a recipe feature. The 2023 PCOS guideline emphasises healthy behaviours without weight stigma. Prefer HPB’s broader messages: wholegrains, vegetables, fruit, lean protein, less saturated fat and less sugar.  
  **Replacement:** “Healthier hawker choices: tips for asking for more vegetables, wholegrains where available, less gravy and less sugar.”

- **“Rich, so a weekend dish.”**  
  This implies moral categorisation of food and may encourage restriction. It is also not medically necessary.  
  **Replacement:** “A cheesy baked dish; enjoy it as part of balanced meals.”

- **“a small bowl”**  
  This is reasonable portion guidance because the granola contains dates and maple syrup, but avoid repeated portion policing across the app. Keep the wording neutral.  
  **Replacement:** “Sweetened with dates and maple syrup; have a moderate portion with fruit or yoghurt if desired.”

- **“not a score to chase”**  
  Good. Strengthen it to reduce compulsive tracking.  
  **Replacement:** “There is no pass or fail, and this is not intended for restrictive eating or self-monitoring. If tracking food worsens anxiety or eating behaviours, skip the checklist.”

## Pregnancy and trying to conceive

- **Fish and seafood frequency is a safety issue.**  
  MEDAS item 9 asks for fish or seafood three or more times weekly. This is generally heart-healthy, but pregnancy-specific Singapore and NHS advice limits predatory fish such as swordfish, marlin, shark and tuna because of mercury. Tuna guidance also differs by country and tuna type. The app should not suggest unlimited fish.  
  **Add under item 9:** “If pregnant or planning pregnancy, avoid swordfish, marlin and shark, and follow local advice on tuna and other fish high in mercury. Cook fish and shellfish thoroughly.”

- **Recipe cards containing fish need pregnancy notes where relevant.**  
  Canned sardines, canned salmon and white fish are usually suitable in pregnancy if low mercury and cooked or safely canned, but users may combine this with advice to eat fish three times weekly.  
  **Add a general pregnancy note:** “Choose lower-mercury fish during pregnancy and avoid raw or undercooked fish and shellfish.”

- **“Vegetable frittata | Eggs and feta over a tray of vegetables”**  
  In pregnancy, eggs should be fully cooked until yolk and white are solid, and feta or other soft cheese should be made with pasteurised milk and cooked thoroughly.  
  **Replacement:** “Eggs and feta over vegetables. If pregnant, cook eggs thoroughly and use pasteurised feta cooked until piping hot.”

- **“Spinach and Ricotta Cannelloni”**  
  Ricotta and other soft cheese should be pasteurised and cooked thoroughly in pregnancy.  
  **Replacement:** “Ricotta, milk and parmesan with spinach. If pregnant, use pasteurised ricotta and bake until piping hot.”

- **“Birchermüesli ... soaked overnight in yoghurt”**  
  Use pasteurised yoghurt in pregnancy. People with gestational diabetes should be aware that dried fruit, apple and juice can raise blood glucose.  
  **Replacement:** “Oats, apple, nuts and seeds soaked overnight in pasteurised yoghurt.”

- **Alcohol note is directionally correct but incomplete.**  
  NHS advice is to avoid alcohol completely when pregnant or trying to conceive. Singapore advice similarly supports avoiding alcohol in pregnancy.  
  **Replacement:** “Rhythme omits the wine item. Do not start drinking for health; if pregnant, trying to conceive or breastfeeding, the safest choice is no alcohol.”

## Soy, breast cancer and thyroid conditions

- The menopause soy copy is appropriately cautious about hot flushes. However, soy may alarm users with breast cancer or thyroid disease. Current evidence generally supports whole soy foods for breast cancer survivors, but those on thyroid medication or tamoxifen may want individual advice.  
  **Add to the tofu or phytoestrogen bread card:** “Whole soy foods such as tofu and soy milk can be part of a balanced diet. If you have thyroid disease, take thyroid medication, or have breast cancer concerns, discuss supplements or large dietary changes with your clinician.”

- **“Savoury phytoestrogen bread”**  
  The word “phytoestrogen” may imply hormonal treatment. The disclaimer is helpful but should explicitly distinguish food from supplements.  
  **Replacement:** “Soy flour, soy milk, tofu and linseed provide whole soy foods and fibre. Despite the name, this is not a hormone treatment or a treatment for hot flushes.”

## Kidney disease

- Beans, sardines, salmon, nuts, tahini and higher-calcium recipes may be unsuitable for people with chronic kidney disease who need potassium, phosphorus, protein or fluid restrictions.  
  **Add a general note:** “If you have kidney disease and have been advised to limit potassium, phosphorus or protein, check with your renal dietitian before regularly choosing beans, nuts, sardines or fortified foods.”

## Diabetes and blood glucose

- Red rice, oats, beans and fruit are preferable to refined carbohydrates, but they still contain carbohydrate. “More fibre” should not be interpreted as no glucose effect. Users on insulin or sulfonylureas need carbohydrate matching and monitoring.  
  **Add:** “For diabetes, count rice, beans, oats, fruit and dried fruit as carbohydrates and monitor glucose as advised.”

- The granola contains dates, maple syrup, dried apricots and amaranth.  
  **Replacement:** “Nuts, seeds, amaranth and dried apricots provide some iron, but dates, maple syrup and dried fruit add carbohydrate and sugar, so portion size matters, especially with diabetes.”

## Allergies and intolerances

- Several recipes include major allergens: fish, shellfish, milk, eggs, wheat, soy, tree nuts, peanuts and sesame. The cards do not currently flag this.  
  **Add:** “Allergen note: recipes may contain fish, shellfish, dairy, eggs, wheat, soy, nuts, peanuts or sesame. Check the full recipe and ingredients before cooking.”

- Canned products may differ in sodium and bones.  
  **Add where relevant:** “Choose products with no added salt or drain and rinse them where possible.”

## Iron and heavy periods

- **“Nuts, seeds, amaranth and dried apricots bring some iron, which matters when periods are heavy.”**  
  This is potentially useful but incomplete. Plant-based non-haem iron is less absorbable; heavy periods can cause iron deficiency and may require blood tests and supplementation.  
  **Replacement:** “Nuts, seeds, amaranth and dried apricots provide some non-haem iron. Pair with vitamin-C-rich foods, and seek medical advice for heavy periods, fatigue or low iron.”

## Singapore-specific advice

- The recipes are Western or Mediterranean and may not reflect local portions, ingredients or hawker patterns. That is acceptable if framed as ideas rather than a prescribed diet.
- Olive oil does not need to replace all cooking oils in Singapore. Canola, sunflower and unsaturated oils are also consistent with HPB advice. Item 1 should ask about the main added fat rather than implying olive oil is mandatory.  
  **Replacement:** “Is olive oil or another unsaturated oil your usual cooking fat, rather than butter, ghee or lard?”  
  Note that this changes MEDAS fidelity, so label it a Singapore adaptation rather than the original MEDAS item.

# 3. The Mediterranean check

## Overall

Removing the wine item is clinically sensible, especially for a women’s health app used by people who may be pregnant, trying to conceive, breastfeeding or taking medication. However, once item 14 is removed, the tool is no longer the validated 14-point MEDAS score. It is a 13-item adaptation and should not be described as producing a validated MEDAS score.

**Recommended intro addition:**  
“This is an adapted 13-item version of MEDAS, not the validated 14-point score. It is for reflection and recipe ideas, not diagnosis or a treatment plan.”

## Faithfulness to MEDAS

The questions largely follow MEDAS, but several wording issues need correction:

1. **“Is olive oil your main cooking fat?”**  
   Faithful to MEDAS item 1, but less suitable for Singapore. If adapted locally, say so explicitly.

2. **“About 4 tablespoons of olive oil a day or more”**  
   Faithful to MEDAS quantity, but four tablespoons is approximately 60 mL and around 480 kcal. This should not be presented as advice for everyone.  
   **Add:** “This reflects the PREDIMED screener and Spanish study diet; it is not a recommendation to add four tablespoons if your usual energy needs are lower.”

3. **Vegetable serving note is wrong.**  
   MEDAS defines a serving as **200 g**, but the parenthetical says “a side dish counts as half,” which would be 100 g and may not match local portions. HPB serving sizes are smaller, about 100 g cooked vegetables per serving.  
   **Replacement:** “MEDAS uses a 200 g vegetable serving for this item. HPB portions differ, so answer based on the study definition here.”

4. **“Three or more pieces of fruit a day? Fresh juice counts.”**  
   Faithful to MEDAS, but add that juice counts only for the checklist and whole fruit is preferred. Also, MEDAS specifies fruit or natural fruit juice, not sweetened juice.  
   **Replacement:** “Three or more fruit servings a day? For the original MEDAS scoring, unsweetened 100% fruit juice counts, but whole fruit is preferred.”

5. Red meat threshold is broadly faithful. The 100 to 150 g note should specify that this refers to the cooked meat portion used in MEDAS.

6. Butter, margarine and cream threshold is broadly faithful, but grouping margarine with butter is nutritionally dated. Some margarines are unsaturated and may be fortified. This is a MEDAS translation issue, not local best advice.  
   **Add:** “This item follows the original MEDAS wording; choose soft unsaturated spreads over butter where possible.”

7. Sugary drinks item is sensible for Singapore. Include syrups, malted drinks, soursop drinks, coconut shakes and sweetened soybean milk where relevant.  
   **Replacement:** “Less than one sugary drink a day, including bubble tea, soft drinks, syrup-based drinks, sweetened kopi or teh, and sweetened packaged drinks?”

8. Beans three or more times weekly and 150 g serving are faithful. In Singapore, include dhal, chickpeas, lentils, red beans, green beans and soy products where appropriate, but note MEDAS intended pulses rather than local green vegetables.

9. Fish three or more times weekly is faithful, but pregnancy mercury and raw-fish cautions are essential. Also, 200 g shellfish seems unusually high compared with common seafood servings; verify against the exact MEDAS table before publishing.

10. Commercial baked goods fewer than three times weekly is faithful. Include curry puffs, buns, pastries, kueh with high sugar or saturated fat, and biscuits if adapting locally, but then label it as adapted.

11. Nuts three or more times weekly, 30 g, peanuts included, is faithful and suitable. Add allergy and salt guidance.  
    **Replacement:** “Unsalted nuts or peanuts three or more times a week? A serving is about 30 g.”

12. Prefer chicken or turkey over beef, pork, burgers or sausages is faithful, but Singapore eaters may also choose fish, tofu or beans. The item should remain as written for MEDAS fidelity, with no claim that poultry is inherently the healthiest protein.

13. Tomato-and-vegetable sauce simmered in olive oil is faithful to the final non-alcohol MEDAS item, but it is culturally specific and may feel odd in Singapore. It measures habitual use of a sofrito-like sauce.  
    **Replacement:** “At least twice a week, do you eat a tomato-based sauce cooked with onion, leek or garlic and olive oil?”

## “Yes / Not yet” and no bands

This is kind and avoids grading users. However, “Not yet” may subtly imply they should eventually achieve every habit.  
**Replacement labels:** “Yes / Not usually.”  
If the app suggests recipes after “Not usually,” add: “You do not need to adopt every habit.”

## Singapore appropriateness

MEDAS was validated in older Spanish adults at high cardiovascular risk. It is reasonable as an educational reflection tool, but it has not been validated as a fertility, PCOS, menopause or ovarian-health tool in Singaporean women. The app already says this and should retain it prominently.
