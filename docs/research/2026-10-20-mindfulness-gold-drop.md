# Mindfulness in Rhythme: what the evidence supports adding (Gold Drop, 20 October 2026)

Jamie asked for a Gold Drop deep analysis of mindfulness and what Rhythme could add to promote it. This is research and a ranked plan. Nothing in the app was changed.

Phone-friendly version: https://claude.ai/artifact/TDM7r7LnVLfhGMrhagGxgK (private to Jamie unless shared).

## The answer in brief

Mindfulness can help women feel a little calmer, lift low mood a little and sleep somewhat better. The clearest gains come from **structured courses of several weeks with some human contact**. A single short practice can ease stress for a few minutes (in one large trial it beat listening to a story), but a Singapore app pilot found no edge over a sham, and lasting change from short practices alone is not shown. The evidence does **not** show that mindfulness or slow breathing reduces hot flushes, improves the chance of pregnancy, or that one-line prompts change anything lasting. A minority of people feel worse during meditation, usually more anxious, and a trauma history raises that chance.

So the best moves for Rhythme, in order:

1. **Make what exists safer and more honest** (eyes-open and other anchors, a "that felt hard" option with grounding and helplines, short defaults, careful copy). Cheap, and the case for it is solid.
2. **Add short self-compassion practices**, the one clear gap. Moderate to low certainty, low risk.
3. **Add a few life-stage moments that say honestly what they do**: "when a hot flush comes" for bother, not frequency; a lying-down practice for waking at night; feeding and rocking pauses; a waiting practice for the two-week wait. Low to moderate certainty.
4. **Test one optional six-week course with Erin**, with weekly check-ins and an optional practice-together time in the plaza. Strongest evidence, highest cost, and fully self-guided versions often fail, so build it as an experiment.
5. Keep rewards small and reminders opt-in; add an optional "how do you feel now" tap; invite real-world plant care; and only then, with community review, faith-friendly and Mandarin or Malay options.

Do not build: breathing sold as a hot-flush treatment, any fertility claim, streaks or minute targets, long default sessions, practices that mix faith traditions, or engagement-maximising notifications.

## How this was done

Commercial interest disclosed: Jamie owns the app, so engagement claims got extra scrutiny.

- **Literature**: 182 PubMed records screened through NCBI E-utilities from 62 targeted searches, about 70 read closely, plus NICE NG23 read in a browser and the MUIS yoga advisory. Abstracts only unless marked.
- **Expert map** (step 3, hard gate): 32 experts and teams, including 6 Chinese-language teams, a Singapore team and 4 Islamic or Malay sources ([expert map](2026-10-20-mindfulness/expert-map.md)).
- **Three other models**: DeepSeek v4 pro (Chinese-language experts and users), Doubao seed 2.1 pro (Malay, Muslim and multicultural Singapore), both blind to any draft. Its 12 most decisive additions were re-fetched from PubMed before use.
- **Claim ledger** (step 5): 70 rows with the exact passage, limits and permitted wording ([claim ledger](2026-10-20-mindfulness/claim-ledger.md)).
- **What did not survive checking**: Doubao named nine regional experts and several statistics that do not exist on PubMed or the web; none are used ([Doubao's answer, flagged](2026-10-20-mindfulness/doubao-perspective.md)). ## What Rhythme already does

The mat offers Erin's guided 3 and 6 minute sessions (she is a certified MBSR teacher), 2 and 5 minute paced breathing, the Koi Pond breath game and nine everyday practices (teeth, shower, tea, dishes, bed, plants, pets, outfit, stretch). After every log the avatar acts in her own outfit with one noticing line. Practice earns 2 hearts, at most 6 a day, and there are no streaks. Erin's lines already teach letting be ("There is nothing to fix right now", "When your mind wanders, that is not a mistake"). There is a stop button but no spoken permission to open the eyes, no other anchor than the breath, no aftercare if a session felt bad, no self-compassion practice, no lying-down practice for night waking and no structured course.

## What the evidence says

Certainty uses GRADE-style words: high, moderate, low, very low.

### Apps and courses in general
- Mindfulness apps beat doing nothing by a small margin: stress g 0.46, anxiety 0.28, depression 0.33 across 34 trials (Gál 2021, L1). Across 176 mental-health app trials, effects are small (depression g 0.28) and stress effects fall from 0.27 to 0.10 under a publication-bias sensitivity model (Linardon 2024, L2). Against active alternatives mindfulness is usually similar, not better (Goldberg 2022, 44 meta-analyses, L3). **Moderate.**
- Structured courses carry the strongest results: online MBCT with light coaching reduced residual depression (Segal 2020, L31), MBCT cuts depressive relapse (HR 0.69, Kuyken 2016, L32), a hybrid digital course run by trainee instructors helped 1,281 distressed adults in China (d -0.53, Chen 2026, L52), and online postpartum programmes of 5 to 8 weeks, especially with support, helped low mood (Liu 2025, L14). **Moderate for guided courses.**
- But fully self-guided versions often fail. A Singapore smartphone pilot found no advantage over a sham (Sparacio 2025, L57); a 219-woman self-guided pregnancy trial found no group difference (Hulsbosch 2023, L64). **Low for self-guided courses.**
- Teacher and group factors predicted improvement more than minutes practised (Canby 2020, L18). Meditation length and type made no difference to wellbeing (Fincham 2023, L61).

### Short practices and micro moments
- Brief training (single sessions up to two weeks) has a small effect, g 0.21, about 0.04 after a bias adjustment (Schumer 2018, L16). A single 5 to 10 minute exercise lowers stress in the moment versus listening to a story, body scan most (d -0.56, Sparacio 2024, L63). **Low** for lasting benefit; **moderate** for a few minutes of calm.
- No trial tests one-line prompts like Rhythme's moments. Treat them as kindness in the interface, not treatment.
- A two-week app course of mindfulness or gratitude did nothing for parents of young children versus a food diary control (Ratcliff 2025, L39).

### Acceptance and self-compassion
- In dismantling trials, training attention plus acceptance lowered cortisol reactivity and loneliness; attention alone did not (Lindsay 2018, 2019, L29). A strong design principle, not proof of a universal ingredient.
- Self-compassion programmes have small to medium effects on low mood, anxiety and stress, with high risk of bias, and a medium effect on self-criticism (g 0.51) (Han 2023, Wakelin 2022, L28). In Hong Kong, only the self-compassion and CBT apps raised self-compassion; the mindfulness app did not (Mak 2018, L55). A three-week self-compassion practice reduced body shame in pregnancy and after birth (Papini 2022 pilot, L44). **Low to moderate.**

### Life stages
- **Trying to conceive**: infertility trials report large effects that look too large to trust (SMD -2.25, Wen 2024, L20). A guided IVF programme helped mood and sleep but not anxiety, infertility stress or pregnancy rate (Bai 2019, Codex lead, L67); brief meditation did not change IVF pregnancy rates (Nunes 2024, L41). **Very low to low.** No fertility claim is possible.
- **After a birth**: online programmes help postpartum low mood (SMD -0.53) and anxiety, not stress (Liu 2025, L14). The well-known smartphone pregnancy trial's benefit did not last to six weeks postpartum (Sun 2021, L66). A couples programme on WeChat cut women's depression in pregnancy (d 0.65, Tian 2025, L40). Nothing tests breastfeeding moments. **Low.**
- **Perimenopause and after menopause**: MBSR made hot flushes less bothersome (15% vs 7% less bother), not less intense (Carmody 2011, waitlist, L11), and against active education it eased anxiety and low mood but not hot flushes (Wong 2018, L59). Paced breathing did not reduce hot flushes and did worse than music (Carpenter 2013, Huang 2015, L10). NAMS 2023 does **not** recommend mindfulness or paced breathing for hot flushes (L58). NICE recommends menopause-specific CBT for hot flushes, related low mood and sleep problems (NG23 1.5.2, 1.5.22, 1.5.24, updated 15 April 2026, L50), and a self-guided CBT app helped in a pilot (Stute 2026, L65). **Moderate** that mindfulness does not treat hot flushes; **low to moderate** that it eases the distress around them.
- **Sleep**: mindfulness improves sleep quality more than attention controls, with no significant difference from standard sleep treatments (Rusch 2019, L17), which is not the same as being equal. A Singapore trial in adults 50 to 80 found mindfulness-based therapy for insomnia beat sleep hygiene and exercise (Perini 2023, L51). **Low to moderate.** CBT for insomnia stays first line.
- **Premenstrual symptoms**: limited, mixed studies (Obembe 2026, L22). **Very low.**

### Safety
- In studies, adverse effects are reported by a minority, mostly anxiety or low mood (Farias 2020, 8.3% pooled, L6); 6 to 14% had lasting bad effects in one MBCT sample (Britton 2021, L7); trauma history raises the risk (Goldberg 2022, Canby 2025, L8, L9). These are not forecasts for app users.
- For single brief digital practices, distressing experiences were no more common than with ordinary internet use, but 10 minutes was more likely than 5 to be distressing (Simonsson 2026, n 5,049, L62). **Moderate.**

### Engagement, rewards and reminders
- Trial dropout is 26 to 48% for depression apps (Torous 2020, L4). Engagement did not track outcomes in a clinic study (Chang 2023, L34). Game features did not change effects or adherence (Six 2021, L24).
- In lab studies, expected rewards for doing a task reduced wanting to do it (d -0.40), while positive feedback increased it (d 0.33) (Deci 1999, L25). Not tested in adult apps, so neither "rewards work" nor "rewards backfire" is established.
- Reminders moderated how much people learned from mindfulness apps (Linardon 2020, L30). Just-in-time interventions show g 0.15 overall, a package estimate that does not prove timing helps (von Lützow 2025, L23).

### Culture and faith
- No published mindfulness trial in Singapore women in these life stages exists that we could find, and none in Malay Muslim women in Singapore.
- Islamic-integrated practices (dhikr, salah, Qur'anic recitation, muraqabah) are growing in research with no standard protocol (Ahmad 2026, L47); a small Malaysian trial of remembrance and forgiveness sessions eased anxiety in women (Saged 2022, L53). MUIS permits yoga for health without other faiths' rituals or beliefs, the closest official position we found.
- Both DeepSeek and Doubao, independently, advised secular wording by default ("正念" rather than "冥想"), no religious imagery as default, short practices, and family-friendly framing. These are expert opinion to test with users.

## Ranked recommendations

Each item says what changes, why, how sure we are, the risks and the rough cost. Nothing here is built yet.

### 1. A safety and honesty pass on what exists (do first)
- **What changes**: Erin's sessions and the everyday practices gain one spoken or written line, "You can keep your eyes open, or stop at any time." Offer other anchors when the breath feels uncomfortable: feet on the floor, sounds, hands. After any formal practice, a small "That felt hard" link opens a 60-second grounding (five things you can see, feet on the floor) and the helplines already in the app (mindline 1771, Samaritans of Singapore 1767), with "if this keeps happening, it is fine to stop meditating and talk to a doctor". Keep default sessions at 5 minutes or less; longer stays a choice. Copy rule for every practice: no promises about hot flushes, fertility, cures or "more minutes, more benefit".
- **Why**: L6 to L9, L62 (10 minutes more distressing than 5), L61 (length did not matter), L50, L58.
- **Certainty**: moderate that some people have difficult experiences; low on how often in Rhythme.
- **Risk**: very low. **Cost**: small (copy, one sheet, a few voice lines).

### 2. Short self-compassion practices
- **What changes**: three practices of 2 to 3 minutes in the everyday list and the mat: kind hands (a hand on the chest or cheek, slow breath), the self-compassion break ("this is hard; others feel this too; may I be kind to myself"), and kind words to yourself. Offered, never pushed, after hard logs: a heavy bleed, a negative pregnancy test, a low mood, a bad night. Postpartum: a body-kindness version.
- **Why**: L28, L55, L44, L21 (shame in infertility), L29 (acceptance).
- **Certainty**: low to moderate. **Risk**: some people find self-kindness uncomfortable at first; keep it optional and short, with the same "felt hard" link. **Cost**: moderate (scripts, Erin's voice).

### 3. Life-stage moments that say honestly what they do
- **Hot flush moment** (perimenopause and after): "Notice the heat, let your shoulders drop, cool your hands or face, say something kind; it will pass." Framed as making the moment less bothersome, never as reducing hot flushes. Next to it, a plain note that the talking approach with guideline support is menopause-specific CBT, with a link to reputable material. **Why**: L11, L59, L58, L50, L65, L10.
- **Woke in the night**: a quiet, audio-only, lying-down practice reached from the bed while she is asleep in the app, so it does not wake her avatar or start games (Jamie's sleep rule). Pair it with one CBT-style tip: if awake a long time, get up for a while. **Why**: L17, L51, L63. CBT for insomnia remains the first-line treatment.
- **Feeding or rocking pause** (after a birth): a 1-minute practice that fits into feeding, with no extra time asked. **Why**: low evidence (L39, L64, L66 are null or fading), so keep it tiny and make the mood safety net the priority.
- **Waiting** (trying to conceive, the two-week wait) and **after a negative test**: self-compassion first. Never a fertility claim (L41, L67).
- **Certainty**: low to moderate overall. **Risk**: overclaiming; the copy rule above guards it. **Cost**: moderate.

### 4. An optional six-week course with Erin (build as an experiment)
- **What changes**: "Six gentle weeks": arriving, letting be, kindness, sleep, hard moments, carrying it on. Five to ten minutes a day, a short teaching each week, a weekly recorded check-in from Erin, reminders only at times she picks, and an optional practice-together time in the plaza for quiet company. A version for after a birth could later invite a partner (L40).
- **Why**: guided multi-week courses have the strongest results (L14, L31, L32, L52, L54), and teacher and group factors matter (L18). But self-guided versions often fail (L57, L64), so the human touch is the point, and the course needs a planned test before any claim.
- **Certainty**: moderate for guided courses; low that an app-only version works. **Risk**: dropout, cost of recordings. **Cost**: high.

### 5. Rewards and reminders hygiene
- Keep no streaks, no minute targets, no leaderboards. Keep the practice thank-you small (it already is). Consider making it a quiet surprise rather than a stated price. Reminders stay opt-in, at a time she chooses, at most one invitation a day linked to what she logged, with quiet hours. **Why**: L24, L25, L30, L23. **Certainty**: low. **Cost**: small.

### 6. An optional "How do you feel now?" tap
- Before and after a formal practice, one optional tap (calmer, same, worse). Used only to show her which practices tend to settle her, never as a score; "worse" opens the "felt hard" sheet. **Why**: how practice feels tracked benefit (Goldberg 2024, L37), but apps with stress monitoring showed smaller effects in one analysis (L2), so keep it optional and test it. **Certainty**: low. **Cost**: small.

### 7. Invite real plants and real outdoors
- The garden and greenhouse can invite real care: water a real plant, step outside for a minute. **Why**: real gardening is linked to better wellbeing (ES 0.55, high heterogeneity, L42); the virtual garden is not tested. **Certainty**: low to moderate for real gardening. **Cost**: tiny.

### 8. Language and faith, with the community
- Keep secular wording and no religious imagery as the default. Mandarin and Malay audio or captions later. An opt-in set of faith-friendly reflections for Muslim women (for example a remembrance-paced breath or reflection on creation) only if written with and reviewed by a Muslim scholar, a Muslim women's mental health clinician and Malay women users; never mixed with other traditions in one practice. **Why**: L47, L53, MUIS yoga advisory; both models agreed on secular defaults. **Certainty**: low for benefit; acceptability needs local testing. **Cost**: moderate, mostly people time.

## What not to build
- Breathing or mindfulness presented as a hot-flush treatment (L10, L58, L60).
- Any claim that mindfulness improves the chance of pregnancy (L41, L67).
- Streaks, minute goals, leaderboards or guilt-based reminders.
- Long default sessions or long silent body scans for someone who has just logged distress (L62, L7).
- Practices that mix faith traditions, or Buddhist ritual imagery as the default.
- Notifications designed to raise usage; engagement does not equal benefit (L34).
- Meditation scripts written by an AI and played without human review.

## How to know if it works (Gold Drop step 9)
- **Primary outcome**: among women who opt in to anonymous analysis, the share of practice sessions rated "worse" (safety) and the share rated "calmer" (usefulness), over 30 days, against a baseline from the two weeks before changes ship.
- **For the six-week course**: a short validated stress measure (PSS-4) at weeks 0 and 6 for those who join, with an honest note that without a comparison group this shows change, not cause.
- Report harms separately from usefulness, and never let usage numbers stand in for benefit.

## Limits
- Most claims rest on abstracts; full texts were read only where marked. The Codex-sourced leads L66 to L70 were not re-read by us.
- Evidence in Singapore women in these life stages is almost absent; transfer from China, Hong Kong, the US and Iran is uncertain.
- Pregnancy studies do not answer questions about trying to conceive or breastfeeding.
- This is not clinical sign-off. Clinical wording should be checked by a clinician before release.
