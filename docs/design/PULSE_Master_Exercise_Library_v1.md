# PULSE Master Exercise Library
**Version:** 1.0 draft  
**Purpose:** Product, content, and recommendation-engine working document  
**Status:** Draft for qualified exercise-professional review before activation

---

## 1. Product concept

PULSE is a short movement-break feature designed for everyday gaps: while waiting for the kettle, between meetings, while waiting in a queue, or during a safe pause at home.

It follows **Awareness → Understanding → Action**:

1. **Awareness:** The user may log cycle day, symptoms, sleep, stress, energy, or other optional context.
2. **Understanding:** The app briefly explains why a movement is suggested.
3. **Action:** The user can start a short movement, swap it, skip it, and optionally record how it felt.

PULSE must work without health data. Personalisation is optional and should improve relevance—not become a barrier to use.

**Product promise:** “Wherever you are, there's a small, sensible way to move.”

PULSE is a movement prompt, not a replacement for a complete exercise programme. A short movement break can support activity habits, movement variety, and practice, but it should not be represented as sufficient on its own to deliver guideline-level exercise, build substantial strength, increase bone density, or extend lifespan.

## 2. Organise by setting; classify by movement science

The primary user interface should help people find a movement that fits their current setting. Muscle groups and movement patterns should power tagging, coverage checks, and recommendations behind the scenes.

### Setting taxonomy

1. Walking or commuting
2. Waiting for a bus or train
3. At work or in a meeting
4. In the kitchen
5. In the bathroom or while getting ready
6. During household tasks
7. In a queue or lift lobby
8. At home or beside the bed

A setting is a context, not a guarantee that movement is safe. The app should first confirm that the user has enough space, a stable surface where needed, and freedom to move without distracting from travel or another task.

### Movement-science taxonomy

- Lower-body strength
- Hip and posterior-chain movement
- Upper-body push
- Upper-body pull
- Core and trunk control
- Balance and neuromuscular control
- Power, impact, and bone-loading movement
- Mobility
- Cardiovascular movement

Each exercise can have multiple tags. The recommendation engine should aim for a balanced mix over time, without pretending that a 60-second exercise equals a full strength or bone-health session.

## 3. Standard timing template

**Default session: 60 seconds**

- **0–5 seconds — Set up:** Check surroundings, find support if needed, and read the cue.
- **5–50 seconds — Move:** Perform controlled, comfortable repetitions or hold the position.
- **50–60 seconds — Reset:** Return to a comfortable position, breathe normally, and note how it felt if desired.

**Short fallback:** A 20-second version is acceptable when time or energy is limited. Do not rush repetitions to fit the timer. Balance holds may be shorter than 45 seconds, and walking suggestions are only appropriate where the user can walk safely without obstructing or distracting from their journey.

## 4. Evidence and confidence framework

Use an internal evidence label to distinguish general exercise principles from claims about this exact product dose. These labels are pragmatic internal conventions, not a formal GRADE assessment.

- **A — Strong general principle:** Supported by public-health guidance or substantial evidence for the broader behaviour.
- **B — Established exercise principle:** The movement is a recognised exercise or practice, but the effect of this exact 60-second dose is not established.
- **C — Plausible movement break:** May support movement awareness, comfort, posture variation, or habit formation; direct evidence for a specific health outcome from a 60-second dose is limited.

The library must not imply that every movement, duration, or recommendation rule has been independently validated. Research on brief vigorous intermittent lifestyle activity is observational and does not validate every PULSE exercise or dose.

### Evidence anchors for the product team

- World Health Organization, *WHO Guidelines on Physical Activity and Sedentary Behaviour* (2020): https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240015128
- US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, physical activity guidance: https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/
- Stamatakis et al., “Association of wearable device-measured vigorous intermittent lifestyle physical activity with mortality,” *Nature Medicine* (2022): https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-022-02100-x
- Royal Osteoporosis Society, exercise and osteoporosis information: https://theros.org.uk/information-and-support/bone-health/exercise-for-bones/
- McNulty et al., “The Effects of Menstrual Cycle Phase on Exercise Performance in Eumenorrheic Women: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis,” *Sports Medicine* (2020): https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-020-01319-3

These sources provide context for physical activity, bone health, brief activity, and menstrual-cycle/exercise evidence. They do **not** validate the PULSE scoring or recommendation algorithm.

## 6. Detailed master exercise records

### P01 — Sit-to-Stand

- **Primary category:** Lower-body strength
- **Movement pattern:** Squat / chair transfer
- **Primary muscles:** Quadriceps, gluteus maximus
- **Secondary muscles:** Trunk stabilisers; hip and ankle stabilisers
- **Functional targets:** Repeated chair transfers, lower-body movement practice
- **Recommended settings:** Work/meetings, kitchen, household tasks, home/bedside
- **Default duration:** 60 seconds
- **Timing:** 5 sec setup / up to 45 sec controlled repetitions / 10 sec reset
- **Equipment:** Stable chair; hands optional
- **Impact level:** Low
- **Balance demand:** Moderate
- **Levels:**
  - Easy: Use a higher, stable chair and push lightly from armrests or thighs if needed.
  - Standard: Stand and sit with control, using hands only if needed.
  - Harder: Use a slightly lower stable chair or a slower lowering phase, only if comfortable and safe.
- **Safety flags:** Acute or worsening knee/hip/back pain; dizziness; unsafe chair; difficulty standing safely; recent surgery or injury requiring professional guidance.
- **Cue:** “Stand up smoothly, then sit back down with control. Use your hands if that makes it safer.”
- **Evidence rating:** B — recognised functional strength movement; the exact 60-second dose is not validated as a standalone programme.
- **Review:** Exercise professional review recommended before release.

### P02 — Wall Push-Up

- **Primary category:** Upper-body push
- **Movement pattern:** Horizontal push
- **Primary muscles:** Pectorals, triceps
- **Secondary muscles:** Anterior deltoids, trunk stabilisers
- **Functional targets:** Upper-body pushing practice; movement variety
- **Recommended settings:** Work/meetings, kitchen, bathroom/getting ready, household tasks
- **Default duration:** 60 seconds
- **Timing:** 5 sec setup / up to 45 sec controlled repetitions / 10 sec reset
- **Equipment:** Stable wall; no equipment
- **Impact level:** Low
- **Balance demand:** Low
- **Levels:**
  - Easy: Stand closer to the wall.
  - Standard: Stand a comfortable step away and maintain a straight body line.
  - Harder: Move feet slightly farther from the wall, only while maintaining control and comfort.
- **Safety flags:** Painful wrist or shoulder range; recent upper-body surgery/injury; unstable surface; slippery floor.
- **Cue:** “Place your hands on a stable wall, bend your elbows, then press away smoothly. Keep the movement comfortable.”
- **Evidence rating:** B — recognised pushing exercise; a wall push-up micro-dose is not a substitute for progressive resistance training.
- **Review:** Exercise professional review recommended before release.

### P03 — Standing Hip Hinge

- **Primary category:** Hip / posterior chain
- **Movement pattern:** Hip hinge
- **Primary muscles:** Gluteus maximus, hamstrings
- **Secondary muscles:** Spinal extensors and trunk stabilisers
- **Functional targets:** Hip movement awareness; posterior-chain practice
- **Recommended settings:** Kitchen, household tasks
- **Default duration:** 60 seconds
- **Timing:** 5 sec setup / 6–10 controlled reps within the work interval / 10 sec reset
- **Equipment:** None; nearby stable support may be offered
- **Impact level:** Low
- **Balance demand:** Low to moderate
- **Levels:**
  - Easy: Practise a small hip-back movement with hands resting on thighs or using stable support.
  - Standard: Push hips back while keeping the spine comfortable and knees softly bent; return to standing.
  - Harder: Use a slightly larger comfortable range with a slow return; do not add load in the initial library.
- **Safety flags:** Acute or worsening back/hip pain; balance difficulty; recent injury; unclear technique.
- **Cue:** “Soften your knees and send your hips back, as if closing a drawer with your hips. Return to standing without forcing the range.”
- **Evidence rating:** B — established movement pattern; exact micro-dose effects are not established.
- **Review:** Exercise professional review recommended before release.

### P04 — Supported Calf Raise

- **Primary category:** Lower-body strength
- **Movement pattern:** Ankle plantar flexion
- **Primary muscles:** Gastrocnemius, soleus
- **Secondary muscles:** Foot and ankle stabilisers
- **Functional targets:** Calf strengthening practice and ankle movement
- **Recommended settings:** Bus/train waiting area, kitchen, bathroom/getting ready (dry floor only), queue/lift lobby, home/bedside
- **Default duration:** 60 seconds
- **Timing:** 5 sec setup / 8–15 controlled reps / 10 sec reset
- **Equipment:** Stable support when needed
- **Impact level:** Low
- **Balance demand:** Low with support; higher without support
- **Levels:**
  - Easy: Small-range bilateral raises while holding stable support.
  - Standard: Raise both heels smoothly and lower slowly.
  - Harder: Increase range or use a slower lowering phase; single-leg variations require separate screening and review.
- **Safety flags:** Acute calf/Achilles pain; recent lower-limb injury or surgery; significant balance problems; wet/slippery flooring.
- **Cue:** “Hold a stable support if needed. Rise onto your toes, then lower slowly. Keep your movement comfortable.”
- **Evidence rating:** B — recognised calf-strengthening exercise; the exact dose is not a complete strength programme.
- **Review:** Exercise professional review recommended before release.

### P05 — Seated Knee Extension

- **Primary category:** Lower-body strength
- **Movement pattern:** Knee extension
- **Primary muscles:** Quadriceps
- **Secondary muscles:** Trunk stabilisers for upright sitting
- **Functional targets:** Quadriceps activation and movement during seated breaks
- **Recommended settings:** Work/meetings
- **Default duration:** 60 seconds
- **Timing:** 5 sec setup / 6–10 reps per side as time allows / 10 sec reset
- **Equipment:** Stable chair
- **Impact level:** Low
- **Balance demand:** Low
- **Levels:**
  - Easy: Extend the knee through a smaller comfortable range.
  - Standard: Straighten one knee slowly, pause briefly, lower with control, then switch sides.
  - Harder: Use a slower lowering phase; do not add ankle weights in the initial library.
- **Safety flags:** Painful knee extension; recent knee surgery; acute injury; advice from a clinician to limit movement.
- **Cue:** “Sit tall and slowly straighten one knee, then lower it with control. Stay within a comfortable range.”
- **Evidence rating:** B — recognised quadriceps exercise; exact 60-second health effects are not established.
- **Review:** Exercise professional review recommended before release.

### P06 — Shoulder-Blade Squeeze

- **Primary category:** Upper-body pull / scapular control
- **Movement pattern:** Scapular retraction
- **Primary muscles:** Middle trapezius, rhomboids
- **Secondary muscles:** Other scapular stabilisers
- **Functional targets:** Gentle shoulder-girdle movement and posture variation during prolonged sitting
- **Recommended settings:** Bus/train waiting area, work/meetings, queue/lift lobby, bathroom/getting ready
- **Default duration:** 30–60 seconds
- **Timing:** 5 sec setup / 6–10 gentle repetitions / 10 sec reset
- **Equipment:** None
- **Impact level:** Low
- **Balance demand:** Low
- **Levels:**
  - Easy: Small, comfortable squeeze while seated or standing.
  - Standard: Gently draw shoulder blades back without shrugging; release fully.
  - Harder: Pause briefly at the end of each comfortable repetition. Do not add external resistance in this record.
- **Safety flags:** Painful shoulder movement; recent shoulder or upper-back injury; numbness or symptoms aggravated by the movement.
- **Cue:** “Gently draw your shoulder blades towards each other, without lifting your shoulders. Relax between repetitions.”
- **Evidence rating:** C — reasonable as a movement break; not a replacement for loaded pulling exercises or treatment for pain.
- **Review:** Exercise professional review recommended before release.

Do not infer completion or a physiological benefit merely because a timer ended. Self-reported completion is a behavioural signal, not proof of improved strength, bone density, or longevity.

## 8. Recommendation engine

Use a transparent, safety-first sequence:

1. **Identify setting:** What is the user doing and what space/equipment is available?
2. **Check immediate safety:** Is the floor dry, the area clear, and the user free to move without distraction or hazard?
3. **Apply known restrictions:** Exclude movements that conflict with a user’s reported limitation or a reviewed safety rule. If a condition is not supported by a validated rule, avoid making medical assumptions and offer a conservative alternative or skip.
4. **Consider optional readiness:** Energy, sleep, stress, symptoms, recent activity, preferences, and available time can influence the suggestion. These signals should not be treated as a diagnosis.
5. **Consider movement variety:** Over the week, gently encourage variety across strength, mobility, balance, and cardiovascular movement where appropriate.
6. **Explain the reason:** Use a short, truthful explanation tied to setting, preference, or recent activity—not a claim that the app knows what the body needs.
7. **Allow user choice:** Start, swap, or skip without shame or penalty.

### Example recommendation

**Your PULSE: Wall Push-Up**

“You’ve already practised lower-body movements this week. This is a convenient way to add an upper-body pushing movement during your work break.”

This wording is appropriate only if the logged history actually supports the statement. Otherwise use a setting-based explanation such as: “A short upper-body movement you can do beside a clear, stable wall.”

### Personalisation principles

- The feature must work without cycle, sleep, stress, or symptom data.
- Cycle phase should be a soft context signal, not a rigid exercise prescription. Evidence does not support universal rules that all women should do specific exercise types in specific cycle phases.
- If a user reports cramps or low energy, offer a gentle option and preserve choice; do not assume the user must rest or must exercise.
- Never infer a medical condition from cycle irregularity, HRV, sleep, or self-reported symptoms.
- Avoid presenting a movement as a treatment for a condition.
- If readiness data are missing, choose a general, low-complexity option rather than fabricating personalisation.

## 9. Habit design and feedback

- Invite users to choose **one to three daily anchors** rather than requiring constant reminders.
- Reward consistency over time rather than enforcing daily streaks.
- Offer a “Just 20 seconds” fallback.
- Use supportive, non-shaming feedback. Example: “You completed 12 PULSE breaks this week.”
- Avoid framing missed sessions as failure or suggesting that users must compensate later.
- Monthly functional benchmarks may be considered only if the test protocol is standardised, safe, optional, and reviewed. Examples for future professional review include a sit-to-stand task or supported single-leg balance. Do not introduce these as diagnostic tests or make them mandatory.

## 10. Safety and governance

### Universal safety rules

- Never prompt exercise while crossing a road, navigating stairs in a crowd, boarding/alighting, standing near a platform edge, or doing anything that requires full attention to travel.
- Never prompt eyes-closed balance near roads, platforms, stairs, or other hazards.
- Never prompt exercise in a wet shower/tub or on a slippery floor.
- Never prompt movement near hot cookware, knives, or while carrying hot liquids.
- Do not convert heavy lifting, chemical handling, or hazardous household tasks into exercise.
- Stop the movement if sharp or worsening pain, dizziness, chest discomfort, unusual breathlessness, or other concerning symptoms occur. Seek appropriate medical help for severe or persistent symptoms.
- Users should be able to skip any movement without explanation.

### Groups and circumstances requiring tailored review

Pregnancy and postpartum recovery, pelvic-floor symptoms, osteoporosis or fracture history, recent surgery, acute injury, significant balance limitation, and relevant chronic conditions may require a reviewed alternative or clinician/exercise-professional guidance. Do not treat this list as an exhaustive screening instrument.

Jumping or impact movements must not be the default. Before introducing power or bone-loading movements, establish appropriate screening, contraindication rules, progression, landing guidance, and professional review.

### Release governance

Before an exercise is activated:
- A qualified exercise professional reviews technique, regressions/progressions, context, and safety flags.
- The reviewer and review date are recorded.
- User-facing cues are tested for clarity and accessibility.
- The product team verifies that the recommendation engine honours safety exclusions.
- A process exists to receive, investigate, and act on safety reports.
- Content versions can be retired or rolled back.

PULSE is a general wellness feature, not a diagnostic service or medical treatment.

## 11. Measurement plan

Track product use and safety without overclaiming health outcomes.

### Core usage measures
- Start and completion rate
- Swap and skip rate
- Repeat use by setting
- Repeat use by movement pattern
- Time to start and abandonment
- Optional perceived effort and comfort
- User-reported reason for skipping or swapping

### Coverage measures
- Distribution across movement patterns
- Whether recommendations overuse easy-to-serve movements
- Whether users receive a varied mix over time
- Safety exclusions and alternative recommendations

### Safety and quality measures
- Reports of pain, dizziness, falls, or unsafe context
- Exercise-specific abandonment patterns
- Confusing or misunderstood instructions
- Incidents by setting and movement level
- Reviewer audit findings

### Functional measures (later phase only)
Potential benchmarks require a standardised protocol, appropriate eligibility, clear consent, safe administration, and professional review. Do not claim that ordinary PULSE completion alone improves muscle mass, bone density, cardiometabolic health, or longevity.

## 12. MVP recommendation

Start with a curated **20–30 reviewed movements**, rather than an unbounded exercise catalogue.

MVP components:
- Eight setting-based entry points
- A small, professionally reviewed movement library
- Easy/standard/harder levels where appropriate
- Safety and context tags
- A 60-second default and 20-second fallback
- Start, swap, skip, and completion logging
- Simple, truthful “why this” explanations
- A weekly movement-variety view
- A safety feedback process

Do not launch advanced cycle-phase exercise prescriptions, high-impact defaults, or complex clinical-condition branching before the rules and user experience have been validated. Expand only after usability, safety, and repeat-use testing.

## 14. Final product decision

PULSE should be designed as **setting-first discovery with science-informed tagging underneath**. The user sees a short, relevant action that fits the moment. The app uses safety, context, and optional readiness data to make the suggestion more useful, while keeping explanations modest and user choice central.

The differentiator is not simply a list of exercises. It is the combination of everyday context, clear instructions, safe alternatives, transparent personalisation, habit support, and a growing record of what users find practical.
